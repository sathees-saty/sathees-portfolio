Business-First SAP Implementation: Show What to Do, Not How to Configure

A successful SAP implementation does not begin by teaching business users configuration screens. It begins by understanding how the business operates, showing the future process, and helping people make the right decisions. Technology teams translate those decisions into a reliable SAP solution.

SAP Program & Project Management · Illustrative, anonymized case study · Business process transformation
The central principle

Show business stakeholders what the future process should look like, what decisions they need to make, what information they must provide, and what outcomes they will receive. Do not make them responsible for learning technical configuration unless that is part of their role.

1. Why this distinction matters

In many SAP projects, workshops become too technical too early. Business stakeholders are asked to review configuration options, transaction codes, tables, condition records or system settings. They may not have the role or technical background to make those decisions.

The result can be long workshops, delayed decisions, unnecessary customization, weak ownership and a solution that is technically complete but poorly adopted.

Configuration-led conversation

  • Which SAP setting should we select?
  • What account determination rule should be configured?
  • Which technical field should be mandatory?
  • How should the interface be mapped?

Best owned by: Functional and technical consultants, solution architects and configuration specialists.

Business-led conversation

  • Who approves a purchase requisition?
  • What happens when stock falls below the reorder level?
  • What information must appear on the invoice?
  • Who resolves an order that cannot be fulfilled?

Best owned by: Process owners, business SMEs and operational leaders, supported by consultants.

2. The right division of responsibility

Business explains need and outcome
→
Consultant maps process to SAP standard
→
Solution team configures and validates
→
Business validates and accepts

The business owns the process and policy decisions. The implementation team owns solution design, configuration, development, integration and technical quality. Both sides jointly validate that the solution supports the agreed business outcome.

3. Illustrative case study: Retail procurement and inventory

Anonymized composite scenario

Context: A multi-location retailer was replacing fragmented purchasing and inventory processes with an SAP S/4HANA-based operating model. Stores raised replenishment requests through different channels, approvals were inconsistent, and buyers relied on spreadsheets to track supplier commitments.

Confidentiality: This is an illustrative composite case based on common SAP transformation challenges. No client name, actual project data, proprietary configuration or confidential implementation detail is disclosed. It should not be represented as a verified engagement with a specific client.

Business problem

  • Store teams did not have a consistent way to request replenishment.
  • Approval thresholds varied between locations and departments.
  • Buyers lacked a consolidated view of open purchase orders and supplier delays.
  • Inventory records were not always aligned with physical stock.
  • Business users were concerned that the new ERP would add administrative work.

How the project team changed the conversation

Instead of asking the business…We asked the business…Business decision / output
Which SAP purchasing configuration should be used?Who can request, review and approve a purchase, and at what value thresholds?Approval policy, role ownership and delegation rules.
Which MRP parameters should be entered?How do you decide when and how much stock to replenish?Replenishment policy, demand inputs and exception rules.
Which inventory movement type should be selected?What should happen when goods arrive, are damaged or are returned?Goods receipt, inspection, discrepancy and return process.
Which output form should be configured?What information must a supplier see on a purchase order?Required PO content, delivery instructions and communication method.
How should the supplier interface be mapped?How will buyers know whether a supplier has accepted or delayed an order?Supplier response process, exception ownership and escalation SLA.

Future-state process shown to business users

Store identifies replenishment need
→
Demand and stock reviewed
→
Purchase request approved
→
Buyer issues purchase order
→
Supplier confirms delivery
→
Goods received and inventory updated

In the workshop, the team walked users through a realistic business example: a store needs to replenish a fast-moving product. Participants discussed who raises the request, what stock and demand information is considered, who approves the spend, how the buyer follows up with the supplier, and what happens if the delivery is short.

Only after the process and business rules were agreed did the SAP functional team determine how standard SAP capabilities, configuration and any justified extensions would support the flow.

3. Ask business questionsDiscuss roles, policies, exceptions, controls and decisions.What must happen, who owns it and why.Business rules and decisions.

5. What should be demonstrated to business stakeholders?

Show the process

Use swimlane diagrams, process maps, role-based scenarios and before/after process comparisons.

Show the decisions

Use decision tables for approval limits, exception handling, sourcing policies and ownership.

Show the user experience

Demonstrate role-based screens and business tasks using realistic examples, not technical customizing menus.

Show the outcome

Explain expected reports, alerts, controls, operational measures and customer or employee impact.

6. What should remain with the SAP implementation team?

  • Functional consultants: Translate approved business rules into SAP process design and configuration.
  • Technical consultants: Design extensions, reports, forms, workflows, integrations and technical components.
  • Solution architects: Ensure end-to-end architecture, security, scalability and clean-core principles are respected.
  • Data team: Define data mapping, cleansing, conversion, reconciliation and migration controls.
  • Testing team: Convert process requirements into test scenarios, traceability and defect validation.

Business stakeholders should understand and approve the process, controls and outcomes. They do not need to own technical configuration decisions simply to participate meaningfully in the implementation.

8. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starting workshops with configuration screens before explaining the business process.
  • Allowing every user preference to become a customization request.
  • Using technical terminology without translating it into business impact.
  • Demonstrating only the happy path and ignoring exceptions.
  • Leaving decisions undocumented or without an accountable business owner.
  • Waiting until UAT to involve business users in validating the future process.

9. How to measure whether the approach is working

The key takeaway

Business users should define the “what” and “why”; SAP delivery teams should engineer the “how”.

As a Program or Project Manager, your role is to create the bridge between business intent and technology delivery. Keep the conversation grounded in process, decisions, controls and outcomes. Demonstrate the future way of working, capture business ownership, and let specialists translate approved needs into a robust SAP solution.
